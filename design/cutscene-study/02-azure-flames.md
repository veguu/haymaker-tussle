## Summary (5 lines)

1. The clip runs 2 s of build-up on the player camera, then cuts to a low close-up that eases out. Then come a 4-frame rush, a side-on contact, an uppercut-style launch, a burst, an 8-frame crouch coil, a 6-frame horizontal dash, a quiet 14-frame hang filmed from below, a 2-frame dive, a light pillar, 15 drawn impact frames, the settle and the fade.
2. Every camera change is hidden: under a full-frame burst, a colour-grade switch or a contact flash. During action the camera never stands still: it pulls back with an ease-out, rolls, orbits and pans. No positional shake is visible.
3. Travel reads through whole-body pitch (upright, then a 45-degree lean, then horizontal), through a loaded pose held during the travel with no wind-up mid-move, and through marks at both ends: a fire and dust trail at the departure, a wind ring and dust burst at the arrival.
4. After each visible contact the target moves on the very next frame: no hit-stop and no slow motion. The only "stop" is 0.5 s of drawn two-tone impact frames, and the scene resumes with the target already far away.
5. For us: load the haymaker in a crouch before the travel and hold it through the dash. Film the dash low, side-on and panning. Replace the 0.75 s near-still slow motion with drawn frames, then resume mid-flight. Do not copy its 10-frame full-screen VFX cover or its knee-bend poses (R6 has no knees).

## E. What it teaches us

**Strongest techniques**
1. Cuts are never bare. Each camera change sits under a burst, a smear, a grade switch or a contact (1.53, 2.13, 3.83,
   4.83, 5.43, 6.07). Because of this, eight camera setups in 5 s never feel jumpy.
2. Silhouette changes carry the motion at tiny size: upright, then a 45-degree lean, then horizontal. These whole-body
   pitch changes still read at 360p when nothing else does.
3. Load first, then travel in a held pose. The 8-frame crouch coil (4.83-5.07) is the anticipation. The dash itself is
   one held "superman" pose for 6 frames, with streaks and a pan. There is no wind-up mid-flight.
4. Travel is marked at both ends: a fire and dust trail where the runner leaves, and a wind ring and dust burst where
   he arrives. The 4-frame rush therefore reads even though the body is a blur.
5. The target moves on the very next frame after a contact. Launches leave the frame in 3-4 frames.
6. A quiet beat before the finisher. The 14-frame worm's-eye hang has small figures, open sky, slow drift and few
   effects. It comes right after non-stop VFX, so the pillar and the impact frames land harder.
7. The drawn impact sequence replaces any freeze. It has 15 frames of two-tone drawings with solid black/white flicker
   frames between them, then the scene resumes with the target already displaced.
8. A cinematic start marker: the cut to a low close-up and the HUD sliding off (3.00-3.20).

**Principles to adopt**
- The camera is always doing one simple thing (pull back, roll, orbit, pan, drift), with an ease-out into each hold.
  It stays still only to let a subject leave the frame (the rush) or to show a big effect (the pillar).
- Keep one screen direction for the whole chase (attacker left, target right / ahead). Use a vertical shot (the worm's
  eye) as the neutral bridge before changing the axis.
- A hold is never empty: in the coil and the hang, the VFX flicker or the camera drifts while the body stays still.

**What would NOT translate to an R6 cutscene, and why**
- Knee and elbow bends (the deep crouch at 4.83-5.07, the low run at 3.63-3.70). R6 has no knee or elbow joints. The
  same read has to come from torso pitch, root height and whole-limb rotation. The good news: the poses that read best
  here are whole-body pitches, which R6 can do.
- Full-frame VFX cover of 7-10 frames (4.50-4.80) and characters hidden for long stretches. Our VFX direction is sharp
  and controlled, and the owner is judging our poses, so we need the bodies visible at the key frames. Use bursts only
  to hide cuts, 1-3 frames each.
- The 3D floor slabs (1.60-2.20): our direction is particles only.
- Halftone in every burst: a style choice. It could be a particle texture, but it is not needed.
- Edit-side extras (the title, the glitch wipe, the blurred portrait fill). Only the fade belongs in-game.
- The first 3 s on the player camera with the HUD on: our sequence is fully scripted from the lock-in.

**Concrete uses in our sequence**
- Lock-in close-up, then the rush: end the close-up with a fast ease-out pull-back (~15 f, most of the change in the
  first 5 f, the dutch easing to level). Settle for ~5 f with the attacker still, then start the rush (3.00-3.53).
- Rush: our current long, held stride filmed from behind is the opposite of what works here. Two options:
  - Option a: keep the behind view but make it 4-5 frames. Pitch the whole body ~45 degrees forward with a smear on the
    first frame, leave a dust/fire mark at the start point, put a wind ring and dust at the arrival, and cut on the
    contact to a low, tilted, side-on two-shot (3.57-3.83).
  - Option b: film it like the second dash (5.10-5.27): low, behind-left at hip height, panning with the runner, the
    body in one held lean.
- Running hook: side-on two-shot, low, tilted, with the camera rolling into the contact and orbiting after it (3.83-4.20).
- Uppercut: the target leaves the top of frame within 3-4 frames of the contact. Next, under a short burst, cut to a
  shot where the target hangs high and far at the top right. That makes the gap visible before the attacker crosses it
  (4.83 framing: the attacker large at the lower left, the target small at the upper right).
- Haymaker wind-up: do not rotate the arm during the rise. Load the haymaker in a crouch coil before the jump (~8 f,
  body still, aura and camera moving), then travel in one held loaded pose. If the arm must change pose, do it on a
  smear frame or under the burst that covers a cut.
- Pre-impact: a short, quiet shot (~0.5 s) with the camera drifting slowly, before the close-up of fist and torso.
- Impact and knockback: replace the 0.75 s near-still slow motion with a ~15-frame drawn two-tone sequence, with
  black/white flicker frames between the drawings. Then resume with the target already flying at full speed, not
  easing out of a freeze. (This matches our existing direction that impact frames are drawn sequences, not flashes.)

1. **Facing at contact.**

6. **Smoothness.**

     A pan that speeds up with the dash. A slow drift during the hang. No camera stays dead-still during action except

     - 6.10-6.43 (11 f): the pillar, the ground burst and the crescents grow.
   - What would look stiff and is avoided here: a slow limb rotation on screen, a still camera on a still body, and a
     freeze before the target moves.
